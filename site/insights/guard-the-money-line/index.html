Financial control

Guard the money line

By Lizeka Mgodeli, co-founder and majority shareholder, SocialBrand · 10 September 2026 · 4 minute read

Most retail fraud is not dramatic. It is not a masked figure at the safe. It is a small, patient leak in the paperwork that nobody was watching, repeated until it is a real number. I spent years in collections and financial administration before I helped build this business, and the lesson that carried over is simple. The money line is only as safe as the controls around it, and controls are boring right up until the day they save you.

Here is how a well-run store guards its money.

One hand should never do the whole job

The fastest way to invite a loss is to let one person receive the stock, capture the invoice, approve the payment and reconcile the account. Not because that person is dishonest, but because a single unchecked hand is a temptation you created yourself, and an honest mistake has nowhere to be caught.

Split the job. The person who captures is not the person who approves. The person who counts the delivery is not the person who files the invoice. This is dual control, and it is not an insult to your staff. It is a kindness. It protects the honest ones from suspicion and it removes the opportunity from everyone.

The three-way match

Every payment to a supplier should rest on three documents that agree with each other. The order you placed. The delivery note for what actually arrived. The invoice for what you are being asked to pay. When all three match, you pay. When they do not, you stop and you ask why.

Stores that skip this step pay for stock they never received, pay twice for the same delivery, and pay full price for short or damaged loads. None of it looks like theft. All of it spends the same money. The three-way match is the quietest, least glamorous control in retail and one of the most valuable.

Capture is not the job. Audit is the job.

As a business grows, the person guarding the money should stop doing the capturing and start checking it. The clerk captures the goods received and the payments. The controller audits that work, confirms the three-way match is clean, and watches the numbers that reveal a problem early. Cash variance. Labour cost as a share of sales. The age of what you are owed and what you owe.

The moment the guardian of the money is also the one doing the data entry, there is nobody left to catch the data entry. Separate the two and the whole line gets safer.

Watch the exceptions, not the averages

A till that balances every day tells you very little. The signal is in the exceptions. Excessive voids, an unusual pattern of refunds, item corrections that cluster around one register or one shift. These are the fingerprints of both fraud and of a training gap, and they look identical at first, which is exactly why you investigate rather than assume. Most of the time you find a process that needs fixing. Sometimes you find something worse. Either way, you found it, and finding it is the whole point.

Why this belongs on the shop floor

None of this is head-office theory. A shrinking, loss-making store is almost always losing money in two places at once, on the floor where product is mishandled, and in the paperwork where the money is captured and paid. Fix one and ignore the other and the leak simply moves. A real turnaround guards both.

That is why financial control is not a service we bolt on at the end. It is part of the same system that fixes the ordering and the stock and the fresh departments. The shelf sells the hype. The system wins the war. The money line is where the war is quietly won or lost, one reconciliation at a time.
